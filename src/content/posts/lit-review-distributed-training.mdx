# Literature Review: Distributed LLM Pre-training

## Overview

Distributed LLM pre-training is a systems problem wrapped around an optimization problem. The optimizer wants a clean sequence of minibatch updates; the hardware supplies a failure-prone, bandwidth-limited, geographically constrained cluster. At frontier scale, the question is no longer just "how do we parallelize one step?" It is "how do we keep useful tokens flowing when links are slow, devices fail, and synchrony turns every straggler into a global stall?"

Standard data-parallel SGD averages gradients across $M$ learners:

$$
g_t = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M} g_t^{(m)}, \qquad
\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta g_t.
$$

This assumes every learner reaches step $t$ and participates in the all-reduce. Tensor parallelism, pipeline parallelism, sequence parallelism, and expert parallelism solve different memory and compute partitioning problems, but most production pre-training stacks still depend on frequent synchronization somewhere in the training loop.

The recent DiLoCo line of work attacks that assumption. Instead of synchronizing every step, each learner runs local training and periodically communicates parameter deltas. Decoupled DiLoCo pushes further: learners are independent, synchronization happens asynchronously at model-fragment granularity, and the system chooses availability and partition tolerance over strict per-step consistency.

## Conceptual Map

- **Classic SPMD training:** model shards and data-parallel replicas execute a globally synchronized program. It maximizes consistency but makes global progress depend on the slowest participant.
- **Local SGD / DiLoCo:** learners run $H$ local steps before synchronizing. This reduces total communication by roughly $H\times$ but still has synchronization boundaries.
- **Streaming / fragment-level DiLoCo:** different parameter fragments synchronize on staggered schedules, reducing peak bandwidth and allowing communication to overlap with compute.
- **Asynchronous resilient training:** learners report updates when available; a synchronizer commits with quorums, grace windows, and token-weighted aggregation.
- **Elastic recovery:** systems can drop or replace failed slices, but if the global program must stop to resize, downtime remains coupled to failure frequency.

The central tradeoff is the distributed-systems CAP tradeoff translated into training. Synchronous SPMD chooses consistency: every learner agrees on every step. Decoupled DiLoCo chooses availability and partition tolerance: a failed learner reduces throughput but does not stop the run.

## Mathematical Setup

In local SGD, learner $m$ starts from shared parameters $\theta_r$ at outer round $r$ and performs $H$ inner updates:

$$
\theta_{r,h+1}^{(m)} = \theta_{r,h}^{(m)} - \eta \nabla \ell(\theta_{r,h}^{(m)}; B_{r,h}^{(m)}),
\qquad h=0,\ldots,H-1.
$$

The learner then sends a pseudo-gradient or delta

$$
\Delta_r^{(m)} = \theta_r - \theta_{r,H}^{(m)}.
$$

An outer optimizer aggregates deltas:

$$
\theta_{r+1} = \theta_r - \eta_{\mathrm{outer}}\,
\mathrm{Opt}_{\mathrm{outer}}\!\left(
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Q}_r|}\sum_{m \in \mathcal{Q}_r} w_m \Delta_r^{(m)}
\right),
$$

where $\mathcal{Q}_r$ is the quorum of learners received before commit and $w_m$ can weight learners by tokens processed. The optimization risk is drift: larger $H$, stale learners, and smaller quorums reduce communication but increase the mismatch between local objectives and the global trajectory.

## Algorithms and Design Patterns

**Periodic local training.**

1. Broadcast parameters $\theta_r$ to learners.
2. Run $H$ local optimizer steps on each learner.
3. Send parameter deltas to the synchronizer.
4. Aggregate deltas with an outer optimizer.
5. Broadcast the updated parameters.

**Decoupled asynchronous synchronization.**

1. Partition the model into fragments.
2. Let learners train continuously and emit fragment deltas independently.
3. Commit a fragment update after a minimum quorum arrives.
4. Wait only within an adaptive grace window for slow learners.
5. Merge using token-weighted updates so learners that did more work count proportionally.

This turns failure handling from a restart protocol into a throughput degradation protocol. If one learner disappears, the system loses that learner's token contribution; it does not lose the whole cluster's wall-clock interval.

## Empirical Picture

The empirical target is goodput: useful training tokens per wall-clock hour after downtime, restarts, stragglers, and communication stalls. A method that slightly perturbs the optimization path but avoids hours of global downtime can win decisively at scale.

Decoupled DiLoCo reports quality matching standard data-parallel baselines across dense and MoE models, text and vision settings, while sustaining zero global downtime under aggressive simulated failures. The important evidence is the combination of quality preservation and chaos-engineering stress: the method is not merely communication-efficient in a clean lab setting, but designed for the failure rates expected in very large training runs.

The remaining empirical gap is external reproducibility. These systems are hardest to validate outside large industrial clusters because the failure modes, network topology, and scale effects are the phenomenon.

## Open Problems

- **Staleness theory for LLM-scale nonconvex training:** local-update bounds are cleaner than the actual optimizer/model regime.
- **MoE routing under partial participation:** expert load and token distribution can drift when learners process different data volumes.
- **Optimizer compatibility:** AdamW, momentum SGD, Muon-like matrix optimizers, and outer optimizers interact differently with delta aggregation.
- **Evaluation of goodput:** papers need standardized reporting that includes downtime, restart cost, and failure injection, not just tokens/sec.
- **Cross-datacenter training:** asynchronous methods make it plausible, but data locality, privacy, and bandwidth asymmetry complicate the optimizer.
