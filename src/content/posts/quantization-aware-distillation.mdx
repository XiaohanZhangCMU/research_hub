# Quantization-Aware Distillation

## What Is Quantization-Aware Distillation?

Quantization-aware distillation (QAD) is a training paradigm that jointly applies knowledge distillation (KD) and quantization-aware training (QAT) so that a student model learns a low-bit representation while being guided by a full-precision teacher. The student's forward pass simulates the quantized arithmetic that will run at deployment, while the loss combines a task term with a distillation term that aligns the student's outputs to the teacher's. The two objectives are not stacked sequentially — they share the same optimization loop, the same forward pass, and the same gradients.

It is useful to contrast QAD with two more obvious recipes. The sequential approach distills first and then quantizes the distilled student with PTQ; this leaves the student blind to quantization noise during training, and the final PTQ step undoes much of what distillation gained. The naive reversed approach quantizes the teacher first and then distills into a smaller student; this propagates the teacher's quantization error into the student and forfeits the smooth gradient signal that a full-precision teacher provides. QAD avoids both failure modes: the teacher stays in full precision so its soft targets remain smooth and informative, and the student trains end-to-end under simulated quantization so it adapts to the noise rather than being calibrated against it after the fact.

The key insight is that quantization noise and distribution shift are best handled when the student trains under both constraints simultaneously. Quantization noise is not Gaussian and not stationary — it depends on the weight and activation distributions that the model itself produces. A student that learns its parameters while observing this noise can shape its distributions to be quantization-friendly, in a way that a distilled-then-quantized student cannot.

Formally, the QAD objective combines a task loss, a distillation loss, and quantization simulation:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{QAD}} = \alpha \, \mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}(Q_\phi(\theta), x, y) + \beta \, \mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}}(f_{Q_\phi(\theta)}(x), f_{\text{FP}}(x))
$$

where $Q_\phi(\theta)$ denotes quantized weights with quantization parameters $\phi$ (scales, zero-points, clipping thresholds), and $f_{\text{FP}}$ is the full-precision teacher. The hyperparameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$ trade off the hard-label task signal against the soft-label teacher signal; many practical recipes use $\alpha \ll \beta$ when the teacher is much stronger than the labels available, and the opposite when labels are noisy but the task is well-specified.

## Why QAD Outperforms Post-Training Quantization

Post-training quantization is attractive because it is cheap: a calibration pass over a few hundred samples produces scales and zero-points, and the model ships. At W8A8 this is often enough. At W4A4 and below, three failure modes dominate, and QAD addresses each.

The first failure mode is cumulative quantization error across layers. Each quantized layer introduces a residual that becomes part of the next layer's input. Because activations interact non-linearly with weights, these residuals do not cancel — they compound. PTQ minimizes the per-layer reconstruction error, but minimizing error layer-by-layer is not the same as minimizing it end-to-end. QAD's gradient signal flows through the whole network, so the optimizer trades off error across layers rather than treating each layer as an isolated reconstruction problem.

The second failure mode is objective mismatch. PTQ minimizes local reconstruction error (per-layer or per-block MSE on a calibration set), but deployment cares about end-task metrics such as perplexity, downstream accuracy, or generation quality. A layer that has been reconstructed with low MSE can still produce activations whose downstream effects are catastrophic — especially in the presence of attention, which amplifies small magnitude differences in logits. QAD's distillation loss is computed on the model's outputs (logits, hidden states, or attention distributions), so the optimization target is aligned with what the model is for.

The third failure mode is activation outlier sensitivity. PTQ calibration uses a small set — usually a few hundred to a few thousand samples — to estimate activation ranges. This is fine for well-behaved layers, but transformer activations are notoriously heavy-tailed: a small number of channels carry disproportionately large magnitudes, and these outliers dominate quantization scales. A calibration set that does not capture the outlier distribution will produce scales that either clip the outliers (losing important signal) or stretch the quantization grid (wasting precision on the bulk). QAD addresses this by exposing the model to many more inputs during training and by allowing the model to reshape its own activation distributions; outlier channels can be smoothed or rescaled in ways that PTQ cannot induce.

QAD attacks all three failure modes simultaneously. The distillation loss provides a global, end-task-aligned objective. The QAT forward pass simulates quantization noise during training so the model adapts its weights to be robust to it. The teacher provides stable soft targets that are less sensitive to outlier-induced logit collapse than hard labels, because soft targets carry information in the relative magnitudes of non-top-1 logits — information that survives even when the top-1 logit is noisy.

Concrete numbers are reported in the literature. The original QAD paper (Polino et al., 2018) showed 2–4% accuracy improvement over PTQ at 2–3 bits for ResNet on ImageNet, with the gap widening as bit-width dropped. TinyBERT, with its layer-wise distillation framework extended to quantized students, has been reported to preserve over 96% of BERT-base's GLUE score at 4-bit weights, against roughly 85% for naive PTQ at the same precision. LLM-QAT, applied to LLaMA-class models, recovers most of the perplexity gap at W4A8KV4 that GPTQ and other PTQ baselines leave on the table.

## Key Techniques

### Straight-Through Estimator (STE)

The rounding operation $\text{round}(W/s)$ has zero gradient almost everywhere — its derivative is a sum of Dirac deltas at the half-integers, which is useless for gradient-based optimization. The straight-through estimator (STE) sidesteps this by replacing the rounding derivative with the identity (clipped at the saturation points):

$$
\frac{\partial Q(W)}{\partial W} \approx \mathbf{1}\{q_{\min} < W/s + z < q_{\max}\}
$$

The STE is biased — it does not estimate the true gradient of a discontinuous function — but it produces a useful update direction in practice. Inside saturation, the gradient is zero (the weight cannot reduce loss by moving further into the clipped region), and outside saturation, the gradient flows as if quantization were the identity.

In QAD, the STE is applied to both the task loss and the distillation loss gradients. Two properties make the combination work better than STE-with-task-loss-alone. First, the teacher's soft logits provide a smoother gradient landscape than hard labels, which partially compensates for the STE's coarseness — the loss surface has fewer flat regions and fewer sharp ridges, so the biased gradient is less likely to point the optimizer into a useless direction. Second, the distillation loss is computed on continuous-valued teacher outputs rather than discrete labels, so the gradient through the student's quantized output is well-defined even when the quantized logits would otherwise produce a degenerate one-hot cross-entropy.

Several STE variants have proved useful in QAD. Clipped STE zeroes the gradient outside the quantization range, which prevents wasted updates on saturated weights. Temperature-scaled STE introduces a smoothing factor that interpolates between rounding and identity, useful early in training when the model has not yet adapted to quantization noise. Learned step-size quantization (LSQ) makes the step size $s$ a trainable parameter with its own gradient computed via STE — this consistently outperforms fixed-step STE, because the scale adapts to the weight distribution as it shifts during training, and the gradient through $s$ couples scale updates to the end-task loss.

### Soft Targets from the Teacher

The distillation loss uses the teacher's softened logits:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}} = D_{\text{KL}}\!\left(\sigma(\mathbf{z}_S / \tau) \,\|\, \sigma(\mathbf{z}_T / \tau)\right)
$$

where $\tau$ is the temperature, $\sigma$ is the softmax, and $\mathbf{z}_S, \mathbf{z}_T$ are the student's and teacher's pre-softmax logits respectively. Higher $\tau$ flattens the distributions: a temperature of $\tau = 1$ reproduces the original softmax, while $\tau \gg 1$ produces a distribution close to uniform. The point of raising the temperature is to expose inter-class structure — the relative magnitudes of non-top-1 logits, which encode the teacher's beliefs about which classes are similar.

For QAD specifically, soft targets serve three roles. They provide a non-trivial gradient signal even when the quantized student's logits are in the wrong rank order, because the KL divergence depends on the whole distribution rather than on the argmax. They regularize the student away from overconfident predictions that quantization noise would otherwise destabilize — a student that learns calibrated, lower-confidence outputs is more robust to the random perturbations that quantized arithmetic introduces. And they encode dark knowledge: the inter-class similarity structure that helps the quantized model maintain nuanced behavior on inputs that would otherwise be misclassified outright.

A practical detail: the loss is typically scaled by $\tau^2$ when combined with a hard-label term, so that the gradient magnitudes remain comparable across temperatures. Without this scaling, raising $\tau$ shrinks the softmax derivatives and effectively reduces the distillation learning rate.

### Layer-Wise Feature Matching

Beyond logit-level distillation, QAD methods match intermediate representations:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{feat}} = \sum_{l} \lambda_l \, \| h_S^{(l)} - M_l(h_T^{(l)}) \|^2
$$

where $h_S^{(l)}$ and $h_T^{(l)}$ are the student's and teacher's hidden states at layer $l$, and $M_l$ is a learnable projection — typically a linear layer — that maps the teacher's hidden state into the student's dimension. The weights $\lambda_l$ control which layers contribute most; deeper layers usually carry more task-specific structure and warrant higher weights, but early layers benefit from matching too, because their errors propagate.

This is the core of TinyBERT's two-stage distillation and is especially valuable under quantization for three reasons. Early layers in quantized models accumulate error fastest, because their outputs feed every subsequent layer; matching their features directly constrains this cascade at its source. Attention distributions carry structural knowledge — which tokens attend to which — that logit-level matching alone cannot transfer, and a quantized student left to recover attention structure from logits alone tends to develop blurred, low-precision attention. And the projection $M_l$ absorbs dimension mismatches from both architecture differences (the student may be narrower than the teacher) and quantization-induced distribution shifts (the student's hidden states have a slightly different scale than the teacher's even at the same dimension).

TinyBERT specifically matches three quantities per layer: the hidden state itself, the attention scores, and the attention output. Subsequent QAD recipes have varied this — some match only attention, some match value vectors, some match the post-MLP residual stream — but the principle is the same: constrain the internal representations, not just the output.

### Progressive Quantization and Distillation

Some QAD variants progressively reduce the bit-width during training: the model starts at 8-bit, trains to convergence under distillation, then steps down to 6-bit, then 4-bit, then potentially lower. This curriculum approach has three benefits.

It gives the model a smoother optimization landscape. At 8-bit, quantization noise is small enough that the optimization problem looks nearly continuous; the student learns to imitate the teacher under mild perturbation. As the bit-width drops, the landscape becomes rougher, but the model is starting from a good initialization rather than from random weights, so it has a chance to adapt.

It lets the distillation loss guide the transition at each precision drop. When the bit-width changes, the quantization grid changes, and the model's outputs shift abruptly. The teacher's soft targets pull the student back toward the correct distribution after each shift, preventing the catastrophic divergence that naive QAT can suffer when bit-width is reduced too quickly.

It reduces the shock of sudden precision reduction. Direct 4-bit QAT from a full-precision initialization often diverges or converges to poor local minima; progressive annealing produces consistently better final models at the same compute budget.

## Representative Papers

### QAD — Polino, Pascanu, Alistarh (2018)

"Model Compression via Distillation and Quantization" — the foundational paper. Polino et al. propose training a quantized student with distillation from a full-precision teacher and show that combining KD and QAT is strictly better than either alone at aggressive bit-widths (2–3 bits). The paper introduces a differentiable quantization scheme — quantization points are themselves trainable — and combines it with knowledge distillation on the soft logits. Experiments cover image classification (CIFAR, ImageNet) with ResNet and wide-ResNet backbones, and the central finding is that the gap between PTQ and QAD widens as the bit-width shrinks. This is the paper that named the technique.

### TinyBERT — Jiao et al. (2020)

"TinyBERT: Distilling BERT for Natural Language Understanding" — the canonical example of layer-wise distillation for transformers. TinyBERT applies a two-stage distillation: general distillation on the pre-training corpus, and task-specific distillation on the fine-tuning dataset. The distillation loss matches embeddings, attention matrices, hidden states, and prediction logits between teacher and student. Although TinyBERT in its original form is a full-precision student, the framework extends naturally to quantized students by adding QAT to the student's forward pass; this combination has become the standard recipe for compressing BERT-style models for edge deployment, and reaches near-full-precision GLUE scores at 4-bit weights.

### LLM-QAT — Liu et al. (2024)

"LLM-QAT: Data-Free Quantization Aware Training for Large Language Models" — the paper that scaled QAD to LLMs. The central problem is that LLM pretraining data is often unavailable (proprietary, contaminated, or simply too large to reuse), and QAT requires training data. LLM-QAT solves this by using the full-precision teacher to generate synthetic training data: the teacher samples a corpus, the student trains on the teacher's outputs under quantized arithmetic. The paper handles W4A8KV4 quantization — weights at 4 bits, activations at 8 bits, KV cache at 4 bits — and shows that data-free distillation preserves downstream accuracy where PTQ baselines fail. The key practical insight is that the teacher's own generations are sufficient training data for the student, which makes QAD applicable to any model whose original training data is inaccessible.

### EfficientQAT — Chen et al. (2024)

"EfficientQAT: Efficient Quantization-Aware Training for Large Language Models" — addresses the cost problem. Full end-to-end QAT on a 70B model is prohibitively expensive: storing optimizer state, activations, and gradients for all parameters at full precision is impossible on accessible hardware. EfficientQAT splits the problem into two stages: block-wise all-parameter training (one transformer block at a time, distilling from the teacher's intermediate activations) and end-to-end quantization-parameter tuning (only the quantization parameters — scales and zero-points — are updated globally). The block-wise stage uses distillation at the block boundary, making the local objective consistent with the global one, and the end-to-end stage is cheap because only a small number of parameters move. EfficientQAT shows that this two-stage scheme matches full QAT quality at a fraction of the cost.

### GPTQ + Distillation Hybrids

A growing line of work combines GPTQ-style PTQ with light-touch distillation to recover the accuracy that aggressive PTQ leaves behind. The pattern is: apply GPTQ for initial weight quantization (cheap, calibration-based, no training), identify the layers where the quantization error is largest, and fine-tune those layers with a distillation objective against the full-precision model. This is a lightweight QAD variant that avoids full QAT training while still benefiting from the alignment that distillation provides. It is particularly attractive when full QAT is infeasible but pure PTQ leaves more than 1–2% on the table.

## Practical Takeaways

### For Edge Deployment

Use QAD when PTQ accuracy is insufficient. If your W4A8 PTQ model drops more than 2–3% on your target metric, QAD is likely worth the training cost; if PTQ is within 1% of full precision, the additional engineering complexity is usually not worth it. The break-even point depends on how much the target deployment scenario cares about the long tail of inputs — a model that loses 1% on average benchmarks may lose much more on the rare inputs that matter.

Start with the teacher generating data. LLM-QAT's data-free approach works well: use the full-precision teacher to generate 1K–10K samples, weighted toward the deployment distribution, and distill into the quantized student. This sidesteps data licensing concerns and is often the only option for proprietary models.

Use layer-wise matching, not logit-only matching, for low bits. At 4-bit and below, logit distillation alone is insufficient — the student's logits are too noisy for the KL divergence to provide a useful gradient on its own. Add hidden-state and attention matching, weighted higher on layers where quantization error accumulates most (typically the early and middle blocks of a transformer).

Anneal the bit-width progressively. Start at 8-bit, train to convergence under distillation, then step down to 6-bit, then 4-bit. The distillation loss smooths each transition and consistently produces better final models than direct training at the target bit-width.

Choose STE variants deliberately. LSQ (learned step-size quantization) consistently outperforms fixed-step STE in QAD settings because it lets the quantization grid track the weight distribution as it changes during training. The added trainable parameters are negligible compared to the model's size, and the gradient through $s$ provides an end-task signal that fixed scales lack.

### For Serving Infrastructure

QAD models are more robust to distribution shift. Because the student was trained under quantization noise with teacher guidance, it generalizes better to out-of-distribution inputs than PTQ models — the teacher's soft labels exposed the student to a richer signal during training, and the simulated quantization noise acted as a regularizer. This robustness shows up as smaller accuracy gaps between in-distribution and out-of-distribution evaluation suites.

Calibration set choice still matters for QAD. Even though QAD reduces sensitivity to calibration, the distillation data should cover the deployment distribution. A QAD model distilled on web text and deployed on medical records will still underperform compared to one distilled on a domain-matched corpus.

Block-wise QAD is the cost–quality sweet spot for LLMs. Full end-to-end QAT is prohibitively expensive at 70B+ scale: optimizer state alone exceeds practical memory budgets. EfficientQAT's block-wise approach, combined with distillation from the full-precision teacher at block boundaries, is the practical choice for LLM-scale QAD. It reduces the cost by an order of magnitude and matches end-to-end QAT quality on most benchmarks.

KV cache quantization benefits from QAD. The KV cache dominates memory at long context lengths, and quantizing it is essential for long-context serving — but the KV cache is also where small errors compound the most, because every generated token reads from every prior key and value. LLM-QAT shows that distilling KV cache behavior (matching the cached representations the teacher would produce, not just weights) is essential when KV cache precision drops below 8 bits.

Benchmark end-to-end latency, not just accuracy. QAD's accuracy recovery is only valuable if the quantized model actually runs faster on the target hardware. Verify kernel support: not all GPUs efficiently execute W4A4 or W4A8KV4 arithmetic, and the kernel landscape changes quickly. A model that is theoretically 4× smaller but runs only 1.5× faster — because dequantization dominates the kernel — may not justify the QAD investment compared to a W8A8 PTQ baseline with mature kernel support.

## Summary

Quantization-aware distillation unifies two compression paradigms — knowledge distillation and quantization-aware training — into a single training objective that produces compact, low-precision models with accuracy that neither technique can achieve alone. The key mechanisms are: the teacher's soft targets provide gradient signal and regularization under quantization noise; the straight-through estimator enables backpropagation through the non-differentiable rounding operation; and layer-wise feature matching constrains intermediate representations that would otherwise accumulate error. For practitioners, QAD is the right tool when post-training quantization leaves too much accuracy on the table, and modern block-wise plus data-free variants make it feasible even for large language models. The trend in recent work — EfficientQAT, LLM-QAT, GPTQ–distillation hybrids — is to keep QAD's quality while removing its cost, and the result is that quantization-aware distillation is increasingly the default rather than the heavy-weight option.
